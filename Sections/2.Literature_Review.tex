\section{Theoretical Foundation}

\subsection{Orbital Angular Momentum of Light}
\label{ssec:oam of light}

In classical electromagnetism and modern quantum optics, light carries not only linear momentum but also angular momentum. Optical angular momentum can be clearly divided into two parts: SAM and OAM. SAM is tied to the circular polarization state of the light wave and describes the rotational character of the field vector, while OAM depends on the spatial geometry of the field—specifically, the spatial phase structure of the wavefront.
At the physical level, the total angular momentum density $\mathbf{J}$ of an electromagnetic field can be defined through the Poynting vector as:
\begin{align}
\mathbf{J} = \mathbf{E} \times \mathbf{H}
\end{align}
where $\mathbf{E}$ is the electric field vector and $\mathbf{H}$ is the magnetic field intensity. Under the standard paraxial approximation, where the beam propagates along the $z$-axis and the longitudinal field components are much smaller than the transverse ones, the total angular momentum $\mathbf{J}$ can be rigorously decomposed into two independent contributions, SAM $\mathbf{S}$ and OAM $\mathbf{L}$:
\begin{align}
\mathbf{J} = \mathbf{S} + \mathbf{L}
\end{align}
This decomposition means that SAM and OAM are completely separate and will naturally decouple and become energy-independent during propagation.

For a beam with a helical wavefront, the complex field amplitude in cylindrical coordinates $(r, \phi, z)$ contains an azimuthal phase factor $e^{i\ell\phi}$. From a quantum mechanical perspective, each photon in such a beam carries a well-defined OAM:
\begin{align}
L_z = \ell\hbar
\end{align}
Here, $L_z$ is the OAM component along the propagation direction, $\ell$ is the topological charge (an integer, required by single-valuedness of the wave), and $\hbar$ is the reduced Planck constant. Due to this spatially varying phase gradient, the Poynting vector generates an azimuthal component that spirals around the propagation axis, generating macroscopic OAM.

\subsection{Spin-Orbit Interaction}

However, when an optical system moves beyond the paraxial regime, SAM and OAM are no longer independent. In a tightly focused system, rays converge at very large angles, causing severe bending of the wavefront and forming a highly spatially non-uniform transverse field near the focus. As a result, the transverse spatial gradients ($\partial E_x/\partial x$ and $\partial E_y/\partial y$) become large, and Gauss's law from Maxwell's equations requires:
\begin{align}
\nabla \cdot \mathbf{E} = \frac{\partial E_x}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial E_y}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial E_z}{\partial z} = 0
\end{align}
This forces the system to generate a significant longitudinal field component $E_z$, whose amplitude is comparable to the transverse components $E_x, E_y$ and cannot be ignored either mathematically or physically. This coupling between field components driven by the geometric boundary and wavefront structure is the core mechanism behind optical spin-orbit interaction (SOI).

To quantify this effect, consider a circularly polarized Gaussian beam with SAM ($s = \pm 1$) tightly focused by a high-numerical-aperture lens. For left-circularly polarized (LCP) incidence ($s = +1$), the incident field is:
\begin{align}
\mathbf{E}_{in} = \mathbf{E}_{0} (r,z) (\hat{x} + is\hat{y})e^{ikz}
\end{align}
Converting to cylindrical coordinates:
\begin{align}
\mathbf{E}_{\text{in}} \propto (\hat{x} + is\hat{y}) = (\cos\phi \hat{\rho} - \sin\phi \hat{\phi}) + is(\sin\phi \hat{\rho} + \cos\phi \hat{\phi})
\end{align}
When the ray is tilted by angle $\theta$ upon focusing, the azimuthal component ($\hat{\phi}$ term) always stays perpendicular to the $z$-axis, so only the radial component ($\hat{\rho}$ term) projects onto $z$. Extracting the coefficient in front of $\hat{\rho}$, the geometric projection relation is:
\begin{align}
\text{Projection to } z \propto \cos\phi + is\sin\phi
\end{align}
By Euler's formula, this simplifies to:
\begin{align}
\cos\phi + is\sin\phi = e^{is\phi}
\end{align}
In Richards--Wolf high-NA focusing theory, the total focal field is obtained by integrating plane waves over the lens aperture (over tilt angle $\theta$ and azimuthal angle $\phi_p$). The azimuthal integration for the longitudinal field component $E_z$ gives:
\begin{align}
E_z \propto \int_0^{2\pi} (\cos\phi_p + is\sin\phi_p) e^{ik\rho\cos(\phi_p - \phi)} d\phi_p
\end{align}
Through a change of variables, this integral can be rewritten as:
\begin{align}
\int_0^{2\pi} e^{is\phi_p} e^{ik\rho\cos(\phi_p - \phi)} d\phi_p = e^{is\phi} \int_0^{2\pi} e^{is\alpha} e^{ik\rho\cos\alpha} d\alpha
\end{align}
The azimuthal phase factor $e^{is\phi}$ is pulled outside the integral, while the remaining integral on the right is proportional to the first-order Bessel function of the first kind $J_1(k\rho\sin\theta)$.

Therefore, after focusing through a high-NA lens, the longitudinal field component near the focal plane ($z=0$) can be rigorously derived as:
\begin{align}
E_{z}= (\rho, \phi, z) = -iAe^{is\phi}I_{1}(\rho,z)
\end{align}
where $A$ is an amplitude constant, and $I_1(\rho, z)$ is an overlap integral related to the lens focusing angle (i.e., the numerical aperture $\text{NA} = n\sin\alpha$):
\begin{align}
I_1(\rho, z) = \int_0^\alpha \cos^{1/2}\theta \sin^2\theta P(\theta) J_1(k\rho\sin\theta) e^{ikz\cos\theta} d\theta
\end{align}
Here $\theta$ is the focusing angle, $\alpha$ is the maximum half-angle, $P(\theta)$ is the apodization function, and $J_1$ is the first-order Bessel function.

The key observation from the form of $E_z$ is that an azimuthal phase factor $e^{is\phi}$ spontaneously appears in the longitudinal field—this is exactly the optical vortex phase with topological charge $\ell = s = +1$. In other words, the SAM ($s = +1$), which was originally determined purely by the polarization state, is partially converted into an OAM ($\ell = +1$) with a helical wavefront, through geometric coupling under tight focusing. This illustrates the physical mechanism of SAM-to-OAM conversion in the spin-orbit interaction.

\subsection{Laguerre--Gaussian Modes}  
\label{sec:lg}

The Helmholtz equation governs the propagation of monochromatic beams in free space. For a beam propagating along the $z$-axis, the paraxial wave equation controls the evolution of the field amplitude across the transverse plane:
\begin{align}
\left( \frac{\partial^2}{\partial r^2} + \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r} + \frac{1}{r^2}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial \phi^2} + 2ik\frac{\partial}{\partial z} \right) u(r,\phi,z) = 0
\end{align}
The analytic solutions of this equation in cylindrical coordinates include not only the familiar Gaussian beam, but also a full family of Laguerre--Gaussian (LG) beams with special geometric symmetry.

The defining physical feature of LG beams is that their wavefront is not a flat plane or a simple sphere, but a helically twisted surface. This arises from the azimuthal phase factor $\exp(i\ell\phi)$ introduced during separation of variables. For the field to be single-valued after rotating $2\pi$ around the propagation axis, the azimuthal index $\ell$ must be an integer ($\ell = 0, \pm 1, \pm 2, \dots$), called the topological charge. When $\ell \neq 0$, the equal-phase surface rotates by $\ell$ full cycles for each wavelength of propagation, forming a macroscopic helical wavefront. The helical wavefront makes the beam's central axis ($r=0$) a phase singularity, where contributions from all azimuthal directions interfere destructively because their phases are incompatible.

The full three-dimensional complex amplitude of an $LG_{p,\ell}$ beam is:
\begin{align}
u_{p,\ell}(r, \phi, z) = \sqrt{\frac{2p!}{\pi(p+|\ell|)!}} \frac{1}{w(z)} \left( \frac{\sqrt{2}r}{w(z)} \right)^{|\ell|} L_p^{|\ell|}\left( \frac{2r^2}{w^2(z)} \right) e^{ -\frac{r^2}{w^2(z)}} e^{ \frac{ikr^2}{2R(z)}} e^{i\ell\phi} e^{-i(2p+|\ell|+1)\psi(z)}
\end{align}
where $\psi(z)$ is the Gouy phase, and the radial factor $\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}r}{w(z)}\right)^{|\ell|}$ ensures that the amplitude and intensity at the origin are always zero. For the simplest hollow helical mode (radial index $p=0$) propagating in space, the intensity profile $I = |u_{0,\ell}|^2$ peaks at a ring of radius $r_{\text{max}} = w(z)\sqrt{\ell/2}$. This gives LG beams their characteristic doughnut-shaped intensity pattern in space, where the ring radius grows with topological charge $|\ell|$ as a square root.

From the standpoint of energy flow, substituting the LG field into the time-averaged Poynting vector $\langle\mathbf{S}\rangle = \frac{1}{2}\text{Re}\{\mathbf{E} \times \mathbf{H}^*\}$ reveals a key relation in the paraxial approximation between the azimuthal and longitudinal Poynting components:
\begin{align}
\frac{S_\phi}{S_z} = \frac{\ell}{kr}
\end{align}
This tells us that electromagnetic energy does not just travel straight along the $z$-axis—it also spirals around the propagation axis with an angular momentum flux density. Each photon in an LG beam therefore carries an OAM of $\ell\hbar$ in addition to the SAM from its polarization. This spatially-derived OAM is precisely what allows LG beams to exert optical torques on microscopic particles and drive them to orbit around the beam axis.

\subsection{Monolayer Transition Metal Dichalcogenides: Crystal Structure, Band Structure, and Valley Physics}

\subsubsection*{Crystal Structure and Broken Inversion Symmetry}

Expressed as $\text{MX}_2$, monolayer transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDs) consist of a transition metal atom, M (e.g., Mo, W), and two chalcogen atoms, X (e.g., S, Se). Structurally, monolayer MX$_2$ consists of a layer of transition metal atoms sandwiched between two layers of chalcogen atoms, forming an X-M-X sandwich in which each metal atom is in trigonal prismatic coordination with six nearest chalcogen neighbors.

In bulk 2H-TMDs, the crystal structure possesses spatial inversion symmetry belonging to the $D_{6h}$ point group, and the bulk material is an indirect-bandgap semiconductor. When thinned down to the monolayer limit, the loss of inversion symmetry lowers the point group to $D_{3h}$. Combined with the loss of interlayer coupling and quantum confinement, the material transitions into a direct-bandgap semiconductor. Furthermore, the absence of inversion symmetry allows spin degeneracy to be lifted in the presence of strong spin-orbit coupling (SOC). Time-reversal symmetry requires the corresponding spin polarizations at the K and K′ valleys to be opposite, giving rise to spin--valley locking and valley-dependent optical selection rules.

\subsubsection*{Band Structure, Spin-Orbit Coupling, and Exciton Physics}

In the band structure, the strong spin-orbit coupling (SOC) of the transition metal atoms—especially in W-based materials—causes a significant spin splitting at the valence band maximum at the $K$ point, forming two spin sub-bands at different energies. This splitting is as large as 430--460 meV in W-based materials (WS$_2$, WSe$_2$) and around 150--180 meV in Mo-based materials (MoS$_2$, MoSe$_2$). Due to time-reversal symmetry, the direction of the spin splitting is opposite in the $K$ and $K'$ valleys—this is the spin--valley locking effect: at the $K$ valley, spin-up electrons occupy the higher valence sub-band; at the $K'$ valley, spin-down electrons occupy the corresponding position \cite{xiao2012coupled}.

On top of this band structure, optically excited electron-hole pairs are held together by strong Coulomb attraction, forming excitons—quasiparticles made of an electron and a hole. Compared to bulk three-dimensional semiconductors, Coulomb interactions in 2D materials are strongly enhanced because dielectric screening is reduced, giving exciton binding energies of 0.3--0.5 eV—far larger than the thermal energy at room temperature ($k_BT \approx 26\text{ meV}$). This means excitons remain stable at room temperature and dominate the optical response of TMDs \cite{wang2018colloquium}.

Two types of excitons can be defined based on which valence sub-band is involved. The A exciton involves the upper (higher-energy) valence sub-band, resulting in a smaller bandgap and lower emission energy; the B exciton involves the lower valence sub-band, with the energy difference between A and B corresponding to the valence band SOC splitting $\Delta_{\text{SOC}}$. In the literature review and simulation discussion that follow, valley-selective emission signals all refer to A-exciton transitions.

\subsubsection*{Quantum Mechanical Derivation of the Valley Selection Rules}

The physical origin of the valley selection rules can be derived from the symmetry of the electric-dipole optical matrix elements. Under the constraints of the $D_{3h}$ point group, the conduction band Bloch states at the K and K' valleys are mainly contributed by the transition metal's $d_{z^2}$ orbital (magnetic quantum number $m = 0$), while the valence band Bloch states at the K and K' valleys are formed by the combinations $d_{\pm 2} \equiv (d_{x^2-y^2} \pm i d_{xy})/\sqrt{2}$, carrying OAM projections of $m = \pm 2$, where $\pm$ corresponds to valley index $\tau = \pm 1$ (K and K' valleys, respectively).

The electric-dipole matrix element for optical transitions is:
\begin{align}
\mathbf{P}_\tau = \langle u_{c,\tau\mathbf{K}} | \hat{\mathbf{p}} | u_{v,\tau\mathbf{K}} \rangle
\end{align}
Defining the momentum operators for right- and left-circularly polarized light as $\hat{p}_\pm = \hat{p}_x \pm i\hat{p}_y$ (corresponding to photon angular momentum $\pm\hbar$), conservation of angular momentum and the selection rules from the irreducible representations of $D_{3h}$ give:
\begin{align}
|\langle u_{c,\mathbf{K}} | \hat{p}_+ | u_{v,\mathbf{K}} \rangle|^2 &\neq 0, \quad |\langle u_{c,\mathbf{K}} | \hat{p}_- | u_{v,\mathbf{K}} \rangle|^2 = 0 \\
|\langle u_{c,\mathbf{K}'} | \hat{p}_+ | u_{v,\mathbf{K}'} \rangle|^2 &= 0, \quad |\langle u_{c,\mathbf{K}'} | \hat{p}_- | u_{v,\mathbf{K}'} \rangle|^2 \neq 0
\end{align}
These strict selection rules mean that in this study's configuration, the $\text{K}$ valley is selectively excited by left-circularly polarized light ($\text{LCP}, \sigma^-$), and the $\text{K}'$ valley is selectively excited by right-circularly polarized light ($\text{RCP}, \sigma^+$), demonstrating a locked relationship between the photon's SAM and the electron's valley degree of freedom \cite{xiao2012coupled}.

In practice, by illuminating monolayer TMDs with circularly polarized light of a given helicity, one can selectively excite electron-hole pairs in a single valley, breaking the carrier balance between the two valleys and achieving valley polarization. When these polarized valley excitons undergo radiative recombination, their radiative emission can retain the valley-dependent circular polarization: K-valley excitons emit $\sigma^-$ and K'-valley excitons emit $\sigma^+$ circularly polarized light.

The purity of valley-polarized emission is usually quantified by the degree of circular polarization ($Pc$), defined as the difference between the left- and right-circular polarization intensities divided by their sum:
\begin{align}
Pc = \frac{I (\sigma^-) - I (\sigma^+)}{I (\sigma^-) + I (\sigma^+)}
\end{align}
Hence, optical valley control relies fundamentally on the direct coupling between the electronic valley and the photonic SAM. The quantum selection rules show that the valley state of an exciton determines the circular polarization of its emitted light. Due to this spin-valley locking, the valley state can be controlled and read out through optical polarization. This strong coupling provides the foundation for integrating valley-polarized emission with nanophotonic structures. It also enables the conversion of valley-dependent SAM into OAM for on-chip light manipulation.


\subsection{Inverse Design and Topology Optimization}

In traditional nanophotonic device design, forward design is the norm: one relies on physical intuition or established geometric shapes (such as cylinders or rectangular antennas) and performs limited parameter sweeps. This approach is severely limited by the designer's geometric intuition and can only adjust a very small number of degrees of freedom, making it ill-suited for non-intuitive, multi-functional, or highly asymmetric optical field control challenges.

To break through this bottleneck, inverse design methods were developed. Inverse design reframes optical device design as a rigorous mathematical optimization problem: first, a figure of merit (FOM) that concretely describes the target performance is defined; then, starting from the desired output, the method searches backward for the optimal spatial dielectric constant distribution.

When dealing with high-dimensional design spaces, topology optimization combined with the adjoint-variable method offers a significant computational advantage. The adjoint method requires only two full-wave electromagnetic simulations (one forward and one adjoint) to efficiently compute the gradient of the FOM with respect to tens of thousands of spatial grid points. Using gradient-based optimization algorithms, topology optimization can freely adjust the geometric boundaries and topological structure of materials at the nanoscale, automatically evolving high-performance optical structures that go beyond conventional intuition in a vast parameter space—providing powerful numerical support for realizing ideal optical structures.

\section{State of Related Research}

\subsection{Metasurface-based OAM Generation}

Mode purity has become one of the most important metrics for evaluating the performance of OAM generators.

Early OAM metasurfaces often used plasmonic metallic nanoantenna arrays. For example, Karimi et al. in 2014 used spatially graded ultra-thin L-shaped gold nanostructures to realize a geometric-phase OAM vortex generator in the visible band \cite{karimi2014generating}. However, purely discrete nanoantenna phase control tends to introduce phase noise, and the excited high-order radial modes often cause multiple concentric rings in the output—an undesirable intensity pattern \cite{karimi2014generating}. To address this and improve mode purity, Guo et al. proposed combining geometric phase with plasmonic propagation phase in a continuous-shape metasurface, successfully raising the purity of a topological charge $\ell = 2$ optical vortex from 64\% in discrete structures to over 90\% \cite{guo2016merging}, highlighting the importance of geometric continuity in the phase distribution for suppressing modal crosstalk and improving OAM purity.

To overcome the loss limitations of plasmonic materials, recent research has shifted toward low-loss dielectric flat optics and all-dielectric metasurfaces. To address the practical bottleneck that traditional OAM ring size grows with topological charge, Liu et al. in 2017 used ultra-thin fused-silica flat phase elements to experimentally generate perfect vortex beams whose ring size is independent of topological charge, significantly reducing the footprint compared to traditional optical systems \cite{liu2017generation}. These high-purity external modulation techniques further enabled optical communication systems: Bi et al. in 2018 and Tan et al. in 2019 demonstrated broadband and multiplexed transmission in the millimeter-wave band and in free-space optical communications, respectively \cite{bi2018metasurface, tan2019free}. On the other hand, to preserve OAM spatial phase features and suppress modal crosstalk between pixels in complex multiplexed image reconstruction, Ren et al. in 2019 proposed spatial frequency sampling and phase compensation techniques \cite{ren2019metasurface}, revealing how far-field interference destroys OAM helical wavefronts and using Fourier-space discrete sampling and fundamental mode filtering to successfully suppress inter-pixel spiral-wavefront interference and modal crosstalk, laying the foundation for high-capacity spatial optical multiplexing systems.

Despite these advances in external cavity systems, purity was still limited by fabrication precision and propagation diffraction. To push past this purity ceiling, Sroor et al. in 2020 pioneered the integration of a TiO$_2$ all-dielectric metasurface inside a laser cavity \cite{sroor2020high}. This study showed that diffraction, resonance, and gain-assisted filtering inside the cavity naturally eliminates unwanted radial scatter modes; even at the very high OAM order of $\ell=100$, high-order radial modes are effectively suppressed and the target $p=0$ mode purity stays near 90\%. Building on this, to further increase design freedom, Zhou et al. in 2021 used a TiO$_2$ all-dielectric geometric metasurface to generate perfect vortex beams whose ring radius is independent of topological charge, demonstrating the ability to independently control beam size and ellipticity within a single element \cite{zhou2021generation}.

Despite the impressive performance of existing OAM metasurfaces, current devices still mainly rely on forward design based on unit structures—achieving phase control by arranging specially shaped nanoantennas. The pre-defined geometric templates strictly limit the design freedom. Although high-purity OAM beams have been demonstrated individually in the above works, device performance still relies heavily on specific geometric configurations and phase engineering strategies, making it difficult to achieve global optimization across multiple light-field control targets.

\subsection{Valley-controlled Chiral Photonics}

The synergy between broken spatial inversion symmetry and strong SOC in TMDs gives rise to the unique spin--valley locking effect \cite{xiao2012coupled}. Circularly polarized light can selectively control the electron valley degree of freedom, and this can be efficiently initialized and detected optically through polarization-resolved photoluminescence spectroscopy \cite{mak2012control, zeng2012valley, schaibley2016valleytronics}.

However, exciton lifetime and valley polarization lifetime in monolayer TMDs are typically very short (on the order of a few picoseconds), which greatly limits the logic operations on valley information and its long-distance transport on chip \cite{schaibley2016valleytronics, gong2018nanoscale}. To overcome this key decoherence and loss bottleneck, researchers have mainly pursued two complementary technical paths. One focuses on using the Purcell effect of micro-nano optical cavities to increase the local photonic density of states and speed up the spontaneous emission rate of monolayer TMD excitons, improving their emission efficiency \cite{mak2016photonics, gan2013controlling}; however, conventional microcavities often lack chiral selectivity and cannot actively maintain or manipulate the valley polarization character of excitons \cite{mak2016photonics}. The other, more targeted path exploits the intrinsic valley-selective circular polarization optical transition rules of 2D materials \cite{xiao2012coupled} and couples them in the near field to one-dimensional waveguide structures with optical spin-momentum locking, establishing chiral valley-photon interfaces at the nanoscale for directional extraction and on-chip transport of valley-polarized information.

The most representative example is the work by Gong et al. in 2018 \cite{gong2018nanoscale}. In that study, a few-layer WS2 was near-field coupled to a single silver nanowire. The transverse intrinsic SAM carried by the surface plasmon polariton waveguide mode due to optical SOC achieves spinmomentum locking, efficiently converting the near-field circularly polarized dipole emission selectively excited in one valley into unidirectional guided mode propagation in that mode, with a directional coupling efficiency as high as $90 \pm 1\%$ . This work successfully established a chiral valley-photon interface at the nanoscale and demonstrated the feasibility of in-plane valley information transport at room temperature.

Although metallic waveguide structures can achieve very high coupling efficiency through strong electromagnetic field confinement, the intrinsic high ohmic loss of metals severely limits the actual transport distance of valley information. To address this loss bottleneck, subsequent research began exploring coupling TMDs to low-loss, high-refractive-index all-dielectric nanowaveguides or photonic crystal waveguides. In this area, the early experimental pioneers of integrating 2D materials with low-loss, high-index all-dielectric microcavities to control exciton emission are Gan et al. in 2013, and then Wu et al. in 2014 \cite{gan2013controlling, Wu2014control}. In these works, the researchers broke away from the traditional constraint of embedding the emitter inside a photonic crystal, instead transferring a monolayer 2D material onto the surface of a pre-fabricated GaP photonic crystal microcavity; this perfectly avoided any damage to the 2D material's excitonic properties from etching, and experimentally demonstrated effective control of the spontaneous emission rate and far-field radiation patterns \cite{gan2013controlling, Wu2014control}.

Building upon these early dielectric platforms, subsequent research pushed further toward smaller mode volumes and metasurface control to actively manipulate valley emission without metal losses. For example, Sortino et al. in 2019 used GaP nanoantennas near-field coupled to monolayer and bilayer WSe2 \cite{sortino2019enhanced}. By using a low-loss GaP resonator with an extremely small mode volume---completely avoiding metal-induced non-radiative and ohmic losses---they achieved photoluminescence enhancement of over $10^4$ times and shortened the exciton radiative decay time to the system detection limit through the Purcell effect.

This series of advances from photonic crystal microcavities to dielectric nanoantennas confirmed that all-dielectric GaP nanostructures can effectively overcome metal losses \cite{gan2013controlling, Wu2014control, sortino2019enhanced}. These nanostructures serve as an excellent platform for controlling 2D exciton emission through the local Purcell effect \cite{sortino2019enhanced}.

Beyond in-plane directional waveguiding on the chip surface, how to actively control the three-dimensional free-space radiation of valley-polarized emission has become another key topic \cite{wang2020routing, rong2020photonic}.

 To extract and separate valley-polarized photons into free space, researchers **must** break the spatial symmetry of the nanophotonic systems. This symmetry breaking creates circularly polarized modes with opposite helicities in momentum space. In recent years, researchers have developed two main parallel paths for out-of-plane control: all-dielectric photonic crystal (PhC) slabs with delocalized Bloch modes, and chiral metasurfaces with localized resonant modes.

% --- PART A: Delocalized Bloch modes in PhC slabs (Wang 2020 & Huang 2024) ---
The first path utilizes all-dielectric PhC slabs supporting delocalized modes over a large area \cite{wang2020routing, huang2024directing}. Wang et al. in 2020 used a silicon nitride PhC slab without in-plane inversion symmetry to control valley emission \cite{wang2020routing}. By breaking the $C_2$ symmetry with triangular holes, they created circularly polarized Bloch modes. This design successfully routed the valley photons of a monolayer $\text{WS}_2$ at room temperature. It achieved an 88\% valley polarization in momentum space and enhanced the radiative rate by 75\%.

To improve valley coherence at room temperature, Huang et al. in 2024 extended this PhC slab strategy to three-layer (3~L) $\text{WS}_2$ \cite{huang2024directing}. Multilayer TMDs have a shorter exciton lifetime, which helps maintain valley polarization at room temperature. They broke the inversion symmetry of the slab by shifting the smaller air holes in a dual-hole square lattice. This asymmetric perturbation created strong directional circular dichroism. As a result, the exciton emissions from different valleys were successfully separated into different far-field channels at room temperature.

% --- PART B: Localized resonant modes in chiral metasurfaces (Rong 2020) ---
The second path uses chiral metasurfaces or high-Q cavity modes to shape far-field radiation. Rong et al. in 2020 transferred monolayer $\text{WSe}_2$ onto a polycrystalline silicon metasurface \cite{rong2020photonic}. They excited the photonic Rashba effect to convert $\text{WSe}_2$ valley spin into highly directional, spin-locked narrow beams in free space.

As nano-optics and 2D material photonic devices have rapidly evolved, research attention has shifted from traditional in-plane directional waveguiding to how to efficiently extract and control out-of-plane chiral radiation in free space. However, building active chiral light source devices faces a fundamental physical challenge: while direct-bandgap monolayer TMDs have very high exciton emission efficiency and intrinsic valley spin selectivity, their atomically thin thickness (about $0.7\text{ nm}$) limits the electromagnetic field interaction strength and phase control ability; conversely, while multilayer materials or directly patterned TMDs can provide enough geometric phase control, multilayer TMDs lose direct-bandgap character and their emission efficiency drops exponentially. As a result, the best technical path for building 2D chiral light sources and laser chips has become the hybrid integration of a high-emission-efficiency monolayer TMD active layer with a low-loss, high-index dielectric metasurface.

On this technical path, recent research has achieved landmark results. For example, Rong et al. in 2023 combined monolayer $\text{WS}_2$ with a $\text{Si}_3\text{N}_4$ Kagome photonic crystal lattice \cite{rong2023spin}. This work broke spatial inversion symmetry to excite the photonic Rashba effect, demonstrating a highly coherent spin-valley-locked monolayer laser at room temperature.

Subsequently, Pan et al. in 2025 used a silicon chiral metasurface to excite high-activity, strongly chiral near-field enhanced non-local quasi-bound states in the continuum (quasi-BIC) \cite{pan2025room}. This work raised the circular polarization degree of valley-selective emission from monolayer $\text{MoSe}_2$ to new highs at room temperature.

Despite the excellent performance of these high-Q cavity-based hybrid devices in boosting chiral extraction efficiency and exciton emission rates, significant technical bottlenecks remain. Existing devices mostly rely on periodic, highly symmetric conventional lattice geometries (such as Kagome lattices or notched square arrays), which typically confine their far-field wavefront control to simple directional narrow beams or multi-symmetric radiation spots. Without an external polarization filter, how to directly shape the spontaneous emission of monolayer TMD excitons into a complex, high-degree-of-freedom wavefront---such as directly generating OAM-carrying vortex beams or LG modes with high mode purity---while simultaneously maintaining high valley polarization selectivity, remains the core bottleneck facing current spin--valley optoelectronic chips.

\subsection{Existing Valley-to-OAM Conversion Platforms}

Existing techniques for valley-to-OAM conversion mainly rely on valley-orbital coupling effects or specific photonic resonance states.

Some researchers have used strain traps in monolayer semiconductors to confine intralayer excitons, realizing single-photon emission with locked photon SAM and OAM at the nanoscale through the intrinsic coupling of exciton center-of-mass momentum and valley degree of freedom, mediated by Coulomb exchange interactions \cite{zhang2023single}. In addition, other work has patterned non-centrosymmetric TMDs into photonic crystal metasurfaces and strongly coupled them to bound states in the continuum (BIC) to form exciton-polaritons \cite{li2023valley}. In this picture, the topological charge $q$ of the BIC is defined as the integral of the polarization axis angle $\phi(k)$ along a closed path $C$ in momentum space: $q=\frac{1}{2\pi}\oint_{C}dk\cdot\nabla_{k}\phi(k)$. This topological property endows the far-field radiation with a polarization vortex structure, and through spin-valley locking selection rules, directly links the TMD valley degree of freedom to the photon OAM topological charge \cite{li2023valley}. This valley-physics-driven chiral control has recently been extended to artificial topological photonic platforms. Meanwhile, a custom Dirac vortex microcavity with Kekulé modulation has also been shown to spontaneously emit OAM vortex beams with high spin-orbit angular momentum locking through the combined mechanism of inter-valley coupling and spin-valley locking \cite{li2026unveiling}.

Despite the successes of these approaches in combining valley information with photon OAM, these technical routes still face the dual challenges of limited purity and insufficient tunability. The strain trap method can produce OAM-carrying single photons, but the random distribution of strain and environmental perturbations can degrade the output purity; while BIC- or Kekulé-modulated metasurfaces generate OAM orders that are highly dependent on fixed lattice symmetry (such as four-fold rotational symmetry), making dynamic and flexible switching of high-order modes in a single device difficult. More critically, the field still lacks a platform that can directly and efficiently convert valley polarization into specific LG modes with high purity.

Therefore, how to break the constraints of existing platforms tied to specific resonance mechanisms and lattice symmetry, and build a valley-to-OAM conversion architecture with greater design freedom, has become an important research direction.

\subsection{Inverse-designed Nanophotonic Devices}

Traditional nanophotonic device design relies heavily on physical intuition and limited geometric parameter sweeps, running into bottlenecks as structures become more complex. To break through these limits, inverse design—which automates the search for optical structures—has become a core technique in nanophotonics in recent years \cite{jensen2011topology, molesky2018inverse}. Among these, topology optimization offers the greatest design freedom: unlike conventional forward design based on unit structures, topology optimization does not presuppose a structural shape, but instead directly searches the optimal dielectric distribution in a continuous design space, uncovering non-intuitive structures \cite{jensen2011topology}. This method is particularly suited for complex wavefront designs that need to simultaneously optimize amplitude, phase, polarization, and mode purity. To efficiently converge in a vast design space, the adjoint-variable method is crucial—it requires only two electromagnetic simulations (forward and adjoint) to efficiently compute the gradient of the FOM with respect to all geometric parameters, dramatically reducing computational cost and making automated design of complex photonic devices possible.

In metasurface research, inverse design has demonstrated powerful multi-function integration and light-field control capabilities. Recent results span high-efficiency asymmetric beam deflection \cite{li2022empowering}, large-NA achromatic metalens focusing \cite{choi2025roll}, full-Stokes polarization imaging detection \cite{wang2024non}, and multi-dimensional multi-channel multiplexed holographic display \cite{yin2024multi}. These free-form devices achieve transmission efficiency and functional density beyond the reach of traditional geometric structures through topology optimization, proving the maturity and advantages of inverse design for complex light-field manipulation.

However, looking at existing inverse-designed metasurface research, the core mechanism mainly focuses on the basic control of amplitude, phase, and polarization of light waves in space \cite{molesky2018inverse, li2022empowering}.

By contrast, applying inverse design to valley emitter control in 2D materials is still in its early stages. Most existing TMD valley emission control relies on plasmonic or nanowire structures designed by conventional physical intuition \cite{mak2012control, deng2021plasmonic}. Although some research has combined inverse design with monolayer TMDs for cavity coupling or dark exciton detection \cite{gelly2023inverse}, high-degree-of-freedom emission control targeting specific valley chirality remains very scarce. Even more challenging: very few studies have explored how to directly convert chiral circularly polarized emission from a specific valley degree of freedom into OAM-carrying LG beams.

Although silicon chips can already realize efficient inverse-designed OAM emitters \cite{white2023inverse}, no valley-selective inverse-designed emitter has yet simultaneously addressed momentum matching, chiral selection, and far-field spatial mode conversion. This research gap is the core problem that this thesis aims to explore and solve.

\section{Research Gap and Thesis Motivation}

Looking at the research reviewed above, metasurface-based OAM generation technology already has mature far-field wavefront control capabilities \cite{liu2017generation, sroor2020high}, and valley-chiral photonics can effectively control valley-polarized emission from monolayer TMDs \cite{xiao2012coupled, gong2018nanoscale}. However, how to simultaneously achieve valley selectivity and high-degree-of-freedom structured light control in free-space emission on a single nanophotonic platform remains an important unsolved problem.

Based on the current state of research, two core research gaps stand out. First, existing platforms combining valley information with structured light are constrained by specific physical mechanisms and geometric symmetry. Current approaches rely heavily on strain traps \cite{zhang2023single}, BIC \cite{li2023valley}, or Kekulé-modulated Dirac vortex microcavities \cite{li2026unveiling}, whose generated OAM orders are highly restricted by fixed lattice geometry and cannot be flexibly tuned; a general platform with high-degree-of-freedom structured light control is still missing. Second, the application of inverse design techniques in valley photonics remains quite limited. Although inverse design and topology optimization have demonstrated powerful light-field control on passive metasurfaces \cite{li2022empowering, yin2024multi}, the core mechanism mainly focuses on controlling externally incident light waves \cite{molesky2018inverse}, and research on actively controlling 2D material emission is still in its early stages \cite{gelly2023inverse}. As a result, an inverse design platform specifically tailored for valley-selective inverse-designed emitters is still absent—especially a design architecture that simultaneously addresses momentum matching, valley selectivity, and far-field spatial mode conversion in a single device \cite{white2023inverse}.

To address these research gaps, this work proposes a valley-selective inverse-designed emitter based on a low-loss, high-index all-dielectric platform, using $\text{GaP}$ as the implementation material, combined with finite-difference time-domain (FDTD) simulation and the adjoint-variable method for global inverse design \cite{jensen2011topology}. This work takes LG beams as a representative structured-light design target, and by inversely designing the excitonic emission of monolayer $\text{WS}_2$, breaks the symmetry constraints of traditional periodic lattices. The proposed design establishes an architecture that can directly convert monolayer $\text{WS}_2$ exciton emission into high-purity LG beams, while simultaneously preserving and distinguishing the intrinsic valley-polarization information of the 2D material without any external polarization filter. This provides an active nanophotonic device design strategy that combines valley selectivity with complex wavefront control capability.
